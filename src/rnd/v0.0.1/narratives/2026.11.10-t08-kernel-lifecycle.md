# The kernel's own life: starting, stopping, crashing, upgrading (T08)

2026-11-10 · Rick Ruiz · Claude doc `https://claude.ai/artifact/HySTtrgtXVg7qKyL9sa3pa` · ruled as D65 (2026-11-10; ten as recommended, the job clock amended to `app timezone`)

## In one paragraph

Every earlier item designed what the kernel does for the personas. This one designs what happens to the kernel itself: how it is started on a host and kept running, what a stop does to the engines it is holding, what it does when one engine dies under it, and how a new version of the kernel is put in place while twenty personas are mid-conversation. The reading of the record shows that most of the hard parts are already decided elsewhere and only need to be joined up: a hibernated persona is a row with no process (the fleet item), a persona resumes its own conversation behind a check (Section 5.4), an unacknowledged message is redelivered after a kernel kill (Kernel-7), and every engine already runs in its own systemd scope outside the kernel's cgroup (the host-capacity item). The one thing that is new is the mechanism that lets the kernel be restarted without the engines noticing: the engines' pipes are parked with systemd for the seconds the kernel is down, which the operating system supports directly, so no supervisor process per persona is needed. The item also gives the kernel a small scheduled-job table that three earlier items have been putting things into (the daily memento verify, the archive sweep, the hourly log sweep), says what the human and the personas are told when the kernel restarts, and settles the disposition of the three systemd units the switch-over list left open.

## What the record says today

The host-capacity item (D60) ends its memory kill with "then the T08 crash rule".
- **What is already decided about a persona without a process.** Hibernation is not a state: a hibernated persona is `idle` with no `process_id` and `hibernated_at` set, and the next inbound message starts the process on the stored conversation behind the Section 5.4 resume check (Section 10.5, D59). An engine crash orphans nobody: the row and its reports stay and the process starts again on the next message (Section 10.5, carried as T08). A persona with uncovered owed work is poked every 600 s while idle (D62), and a poke is a message.
- **What is already decided about a kernel that dies.** The inbox ledger is written after the stdin write and before the receipt (D52), and Kernel-7 kills the kernel between the two and restarts it: the unacknowledged message is delivered once more, nothing acknowledged is replayed, `inbox.json` is rebuilt from the log. The log writer recovers its `sequence` from the newest segment and opens a new segment with reason `start` (D56). The kernel socket is `~/.lupin/kernel/kernel.sock`, mode 0600; the registry is `~/.lupin/kernel/registry.json`, written atomically, the kernel its only writer (Phase 1 spec Sections 7 and 11).
- **What is already decided about where the engines run.** Every engine is launched with `systemd-run --user --scope … --slice=lupin-personas.slice --unit=lupin-persona-<id>` (D60), so the engine's processes are in their own cgroup, not the kernel service's. At kernel start the slice gets its memory bound and `kernel.started` records `slice_bound`.
- **What is already decided about the host account.** One kernel per host; on a production host the kernel runs under a dedicated unprivileged account and refuses to start if that account is privileged (D59, D50 as amended). Credentials are read from files at the moment of use (`credential_paths`), with a startup invariant on their modes.
- **The gate when the kernel is unreachable.** Phase 1's shim allows the call and logs `gate.unreachable` (fail-open, Phase 1 spec Section 10 step 5). Nothing says how long it tries first.
- **The three units without a disposition (T19 spec Section 5 row, D63):** `tmux-execve-killtrace`, `lupin-bounce-watcher`, `lupin-credential-watcher`, to be read before the Move clock. `lupin-arbiter-app` and `lupin-cc-memory-watch` are already Operate-at-Switch (stop and disable; the kernel unit replaces the first, the D60 sampler the second).

## What was found

Read from the laptop clones, read-only: this repo at `eeaca99`, lupin at `82fdc0182`.

**How AH4L is kept alive today.** The seats live in tmux sessions started by `start-cc-with-tmux.sh`, which is why an arbiter restart never touched them: the arbiter held no pipe to any seat. The arbiter's own unit (`lupin_arbiter_app/systemd/lupin-arbiter-app.service`) is a systemd *user* unit (the service account has no root; "cron-only was rejected because it loses the Restart=always guarantee"), `Type=exec`, `Restart=always`, `RestartSec=5`, `TimeoutStopSec=75` sized to its poll loop, with linger so it survives logout, and a comment that `WatchdogSec` plus `sd_notify` was "a V2 add". The credential watcher and bounce watcher follow the same user-unit pattern. That pattern is the house style; it is also exactly what D59's unprivileged account needs, since a system unit cannot be installed without root.

**What the kernel changes about that.** Under the kernel the engines are *children of the kernel*, speaking line-delimited JSON over three pipes each. If the kernel process exits, the write end of every engine's stdin closes; an engine in `--input-format stream-json` reads end-of-file and exits after its current turn, and a busy engine that writes to a stdout nobody holds gets a broken pipe and dies at once. So a plain kernel restart today would, by itself, take the whole fleet down with it and bring it back one resume at a time, each a prompt-cache miss. That is the problem the "upgrade with personas live" line in the register was pointing at.

**What the operating system offers.** systemd keeps a per-service *file-descriptor store*: a service hands descriptors to its manager over the notify socket and gets them back, by name, at its next start. The store survives `systemctl restart` and is dropped by `systemctl stop` followed by a separate `start` (the documented distinction; `FileDescriptorStorePreserve=yes`, which keeps it across a stop too, needs systemd 254, and the development server runs 249). A service can also be given a different kill signal for a restart-stop than for a plain stop (`RestartKillSignal=` beside `KillSignal=`), so the kernel can tell which of the two it is being asked to do. Because the engines are in their own scopes, stopping the kernel service's cgroup does not reach them. Pushing a descriptor is one `sendmsg` with `SCM_RIGHTS` to `$NOTIFY_SOCKET`; receiving them back is reading `LISTEN_FDS` and `LISTEN_FDNAMES`. No library beyond the standard one.

**What a parked engine does while the kernel is down.** Its pipes stay open, held by the user manager. An idle engine notices nothing. A busy engine that produces output fills the pipe's 64 KiB buffer and then blocks on the write until the new kernel reads; a tool call that goes to the gate gets a connection refused on the socket until the new kernel binds it. The first is harmless for a restart measured in seconds. The second is why the shim needs a retry window before it decides what an unreachable gate means.

**Partial lines.** The protocol is one JSON object per line. Whatever part of a line the old kernel had read into memory and not yet processed is lost; the new kernel reads from the pipe's current position, which may be mid-line. The fix is one rule: after a reattach, discard up to and including the first newline, and log `session.reattached { discarded_bytes }`. Nothing in the engine's own conversation is lost by this; only the frame the kernel was in the middle of rendering.

**The jobs that already exist.** In-kernel, on their own timers: the memory sampler every 15 s (D60), the hourly log sweep (D56), the hibernation sweep (D59), the per-persona idle re-check every 600 s (D62). Named for T08: the daily memento verify (D62) and the daily archive sweep (D64). The soak's seven measures (D63) include "memento verify green daily", so the verify has to leave a record a script can read.

**What "memento verify" meant in AH4L.** A daily tick in the Stop hook's owed-work oracle that made a seat write a memento if its last one was a day old. The turn-end item took it out of the oracle and sent it here. What it has to prove is unchanged: no persona has done a day's work since its last record.

## The problems

1. **The unit.** Its name, type, account, restart policy, and how a misconfigured kernel is kept from flapping under `Restart=always`.
2. **The engines across a kernel restart.** Whether they die with the kernel and come back one by one, or survive it.
3. **The stop.** What `systemctl stop` does to a busy persona: cut it off, wait a bounded time, or wait for ever.
4. **The start.** The order of startup steps, and whether personas that were running before the stop are started again at once or on their next message.
5. **One engine crashing.** What is recorded, what the persona is told when it comes back, whether its interrupted message is replayed, and how a crash loop is stopped.
6. **The upgrade.** The procedure for putting a new kernel in place, what state has to survive it, and what the shim does while the gate is away.
7. **Scheduled jobs.** Where they live, how a missed run is handled, how they are seen and forced from the console.
8. **The memento verify.** What exactly it checks and what it does when the check fails.
9. **Who is told.** What the human and the personas learn when the kernel stops or restarts, and whether Lupin's own restart notices need anything from the kernel.
10. **The three units.** A disposition for each, before the Move clock.

## Options and recommendations

### 1. The unit

**Option A (recommended): a systemd user unit, `lupin-kernel.service`, under the persona account.** `Type=notify` so systemd learns the kernel is up only when the socket is bound and the registry loaded, not when the interpreter starts; `WatchdogSec=60` with a ping from the event loop so a hung kernel is restarted, not just an exited one; `Restart=on-failure`, `RestartSec=5`; `RestartPreventExitStatus=78` so a startup refusal (a D50 or D59 invariant, a bad configuration; exit 78 is the conventional "configuration error") is reported once and never retried in a loop; `StartLimitBurst=5` in `StartLimitIntervalSec=300` as the last fence; `TimeoutStopSec` set to the drain bound plus the grace plus 30 s; `FileDescriptorStoreMax=256` (three descriptors per persona; room for eighty); `KillSignal=SIGTERM`, `RestartKillSignal=SIGUSR1`; `ExecStart=<venv>/bin/python -m uhw_kernel` in Phase 1 and `lupin kernel run` after the move; `Environment=LUPIN_ROOT=…` as the one deploy-time value, the arbiter's convention. Linger enabled for the account. Installed by a script that renders the two paths, as `install-bounce-watcher.sh` does.
*Pros:* needs no root, which the production account must not have; the same shape as the four units already on the host; the watchdog and notify close the gap the arbiter unit left open. *Cons:* a user manager must be running for the account (linger), one more thing the host inventory checks.

**Option B: a system unit with `User=`.** *Pros:* starts at boot without linger. *Cons:* installing or editing it needs root; the persona account has none by design, so every kernel upgrade would need a privileged step.

**Option C: no unit; tmux or nohup as AH4L.** *Pros:* nothing to install. *Cons:* no restart on crash, no watchdog, no stop semantics; it is what the arbiter's unit was written to get away from.

### 2. The engines across a kernel restart

**Option A (recommended): the engines survive a restart; a plain stop drains and stops them.** At every spawn the kernel pushes the engine's three pipe descriptors to the store under the name `persona-<id>-{in,out,err}`. On `SIGUSR1` (a restart-stop) the kernel writes `kernel.stopping { reason: restart }`, closes nothing, and exits. On `SIGTERM` (a plain stop) it runs the stop sequence of problem 3 and removes the descriptors from the store as each engine stops. At start it reads the store back and reattaches every persona whose row still has a `process_id` that is alive and in its scope; a persona in the store with no live process, or a live process with nothing in the store, is cleaned up and the row marked for a lazy start. `session.reattached { discarded_bytes }` per persona, counts in `kernel.started`.
*Pros:* a kernel upgrade costs the fleet nothing: no resume, no cache miss, no lost turn beyond one partial frame; no new process type; about sixty lines of standard-library code. *Cons:* a mechanism to test on the VM (acceptance Lifecycle-2); `systemctl stop` and `start` typed separately still lose the store, which is the documented behavior and is what the stop sequence is for.

**Option B: the engines always die with the kernel and come back lazily.** *Pros:* nothing to build beyond what hibernation already does. *Cons:* every kernel restart is a fleet restart; twenty cache misses and every busy turn lost; the register's "upgrade with personas live" line stays unmet.

**Option C: a supervisor process per persona holding the pipes, reconnecting to the kernel over a socket.** *Pros:* survives anything short of a reboot. *Cons:* it is AH4L's listener-per-seat again, which D36 replaced with one process on purpose; a second protocol, a second thing to version and debug.

### 3. The stop

**Option A (recommended): a bounded drain.** On SIGTERM: `kernel.stopping { reason: stop, running, busy }`; the control API answers 503 to spawns; every idle persona's stdin is closed (the engine exits on end-of-file), SIGTERM after `kernel stop grace seconds` (10), SIGKILL after another grace; every busy persona is given up to `kernel stop drain seconds` (180, the family-wide MCP call cap, so one tool call can finish) to produce its result, then the same closing; each row gets `process_id: null`, `stopped_at`, `stopped_in_turn: true | false`; `kernel.stopped { drained, cut, killed }`; the socket file is removed. `TimeoutStopSec` is sized to this so systemd never races it.
*Pros:* a turn that was about to finish finishes; a stuck one cannot hold the host; the numbers are two configuration keys. *Cons:* a stop can take three minutes when something is busy; the console shows the countdown (`kernel.stopping` on every view).

**Option B: no drain, close everything at once.** *Pros:* a stop in seconds. *Cons:* every busy turn is lost and replayed on resume.

**Option C: wait for every turn to end.** *Pros:* nothing lost. *Cons:* one held turn means the kernel never stops; `TimeoutStopSec` cannot be set honestly.

### 4. The start

**Option A (recommended): a fixed order, then lazy starts.** (1) Configuration through the ConfigurationManager; (2) the invariants of D50 and D59 and the one-kernel-per-host check (the socket path: connect to an existing file; a live answer is exit 78 with its pid in the message, a dead file is removed); any failure is exit 78 and one line on stderr; (3) the registry loaded, its `schema_version` migrated forward if older, the previous file kept as `registry.json.v<n>`; (4) the slice bound (D60); (5) the socket bound, 0600; (6) the descriptor store read and reattaches done, every other non-closed row set to `process_id: null`; (7) one WebSocket per non-closed persona (D36) with the catch-up pull (D52, D55); (8) `kernel.started { version, config_sha256, reattached, resume_pending, … }`; (9) `READY=1` to systemd; (10) the jobs table's catch-up (problem 7) and the first hibernation sweep. A persona with no process starts one on its next block, exactly as a hibernated one does: the stored conversation, the Section 5.4 check, `session.resumed { reason: kernel_restart | crash | hibernation }`. Because an owed persona is poked within 600 s (D62) and a poke is a block, nothing that has work waits longer than that.
*Pros:* one code path for hibernation, crash and restart; the kernel is ready in seconds regardless of fleet size; the ordering puts every refusal before any side effect. *Cons:* the first message to each returned persona pays the resume.

**Option B: eagerly restart every persona that was running.** *Pros:* the fleet is warm before anyone speaks. *Cons:* N simultaneous engine starts against the memory budget and the vetting lock; the warmth is paid for personas nobody may speak to for hours.

**Option C: eagerly restart only those that were busy at the stop.** *Pros:* finishes what was cut. *Cons:* a resumed conversation does not resume the turn; the model still needs to be told, which is problem 5's block anyway; little gained over A.

### 5. One engine crashing

**Option A (recommended): record, come back lazily with a crash block, and stop a loop.** The adapter sees the process exit without a `result` frame (or at all): `session.crashed { exit_code, signal, oom_killed, in_turn, turn_id, uptime_seconds }` (`oom_killed` from D60's `memory.events`), the row stays in every respect but `process_id`, its reports stay, its voice stays allocated. On its next block the process starts on the stored conversation and the first block of that turn is `[kernel HH:MM:SS crashed <reason>]` with one sentence ("Your previous turn ended without a result at <time>") and, when the crash was in a turn, the header and text of the interrupted message, so the model decides what to redo. The kernel never re-runs the message as a fresh turn by itself: a tool call may already have had its effect. Crash loop: `kernel crash loop count` (3) crashes within `kernel crash loop minutes` (10) set `crash_looping: true` on the row; no further automatic start; one notice to the manager (or the human when there is none); the operator clears it with a re-spin or a dismissal. A memory re-spin under D60 is not a crash and does not count.
*Pros:* reuses the hibernation path; the model, not the kernel, decides about side effects; a sick engine or a bad binary cannot burn tokens unattended. *Cons:* a crashed persona with nothing owed stays down until spoken to, which is also what hibernation does.

**Option B: restart at once.** *Pros:* warm again in seconds. *Cons:* same loop risk, met by the same guard; the warmth is unused until a message arrives.

**Option C: restart at once and replay the interrupted message as a new turn.** *Pros:* the work continues without a human. *Cons:* at-least-once execution of tool calls with side effects (a commit, a DM, a task write); the gate's single-use approvals (D49) would also have to be re-opened.

### 6. The upgrade

**Option A (recommended): restart in place.** Install the new version (in Phase 1 `git pull` in the checkout; after the move, lupin's own deploy); `systemctl --user restart lupin-kernel`; `lupin kernel status` shows `version`, `reattached`, `resume_pending`, `crash_looping`. The registry carries `schema_version` and the kernel migrates forward only, keeping the previous file; a downgrade with a schema change restores that file by hand (one runbook line). `kernel_version` is already in every `log.segment` (D56). **The shim during the window:** on a connection refused it retries the gate for `kernel gate retry seconds` (30) before applying the unreachable policy; and that policy becomes `allow` on a `test` host and `deny` on a `production` host (`gate.unreachable { decision }`), which closes a hole the Phase 1 fail-open left. The API's own version field and compatibility rule go to T09. Rollback (D63) is the same procedure with the previous version.
*Pros:* one command, no window to schedule, the fleet unaffected under problem 2's mechanism; the shim rule is three lines. *Cons:* a schema downgrade is manual.

**Option B: two kernels side by side, hand over, stop the old.** *Pros:* zero gap. *Cons:* breaks one kernel per host (D59), two writers on the registry and the socket; nothing it buys is worth the design.

**Option C: upgrades only in a maintenance window with the fleet stopped.** *Pros:* nothing can go wrong mid-turn. *Cons:* the fleet is meant to run around the clock; this is what the register asked to avoid.

### 7. Scheduled jobs

**Option A (recommended): an in-kernel job table.** `jobs.py`: a list of named jobs, each with a cadence (`daily at HH:MM` UTC or `every N seconds`), a callable, and `last_run_at` persisted in `~/.lupin/kernel/jobs.json`. One scheduler task; a job never overlaps itself; a job that raises is logged and the next run waits for its cadence. At start, any job whose last run is older than one cadence runs once (a catch-up, never a backlog). Records `job.started { name }`, `job.finished { name, ok, duration_ms, detail }`, `job.skipped { name, reason }`. `lupin kernel jobs` lists name, cadence, last run, last result; `lupin kernel job run <name>` forces one. The first table: `memento.verify` daily (D62), `archive.sweep` daily (D64), `log.sweep` hourly (D56, moved here from its own timer), `tree.sweep` hourly (D59's kept-tree sweep). The samplers (memory, idle re-check, hibernation) stay what they are: tasks on their own clocks, not jobs. Configuration: `kernel job <name> enabled` and `kernel job <name> at` (daily) or `kernel job <name> every seconds`.
*Pros:* one place to look; the catch-up means a kernel that was down at 03:30 still verifies at 03:31; the soak's "green daily" is a `job.finished` query. *Cons:* a job runs inside the kernel process; a slow job is bounded by making it an awaited subprocess where it does real work (the archive sweep, the tree sweep).

**Option B: systemd timers per job.** *Pros:* survives a kernel crash independently. *Cons:* a unit per job to install under the persona account; every job then needs the kernel's socket anyway to read the registry; two schedulers to read in the console.

**Option C: cron.** *Pros:* familiar. *Cons:* it is what the context-pressure poll used and what D57 removed; no record in the kernel log, no catch-up.

### 8. The memento verify

**Option A (recommended): relative to the persona's own work, with a request before a notice.** Daily at `kernel job memento verify at` (03:30 UTC): for every non-closed persona, compare `last_turn_at` on the row with the newest memento record stamped with its `persona_id` (D54's stamp key, the mirror under `~/.lupin/mementos/`); a persona whose last turn is more than `kernel memento verify max age hours` (24) newer than its last record is *due*. A due persona that is running or hibernated gets a `[kernel HH:MM:SS memento due]` block as its next turn ("Write a memento now; your last record is <age> old"), which wakes it if hibernated (it has work to record, by definition). A persona still due at the next day's run gets one notice to its manager. `job.finished { ok: due == 0, due: [ids], requested: [ids], notified: [ids] }`.
*Pros:* a persona that has not worked is never pestered; the request is the same block kind everything else uses; the soak's measure is `ok`. *Cons:* a persona that works all day and ignores the block is caught only the next day, which is the cadence AH4L had.

**Option B: absolute age.** *Pros:* simplest. *Cons:* a persona hibernated for a week is flagged every day for nothing.

**Option C: detect only, never request.** *Pros:* the kernel stays out of the conversation. *Cons:* the AH4L tick's whole value was that it made the seat write; a notice to a manager is a slower way to the same block.

### 9. Who is told

**Option A (recommended): one card to the human, one block to each returned persona.** After a start that is not the host's first (`kernel.started` with a `previous_run` field), the kernel posts one `POST /api/notify` card at `priority: low`, ding suppressed: "Kernel restarted at <time> (<reason>): <n> personas reattached, <m> resume on their next message, <k> crash-looping." On a plain stop, one card: "Kernel stopping (<reason>); <n> personas drained, <m> cut." Every persona that was running at the stop and is resumed gets `[kernel HH:MM:SS restarted]` as the first block of its next turn, one sentence (the kernel restarted; any tool call that failed with a connection error in the last minute may be repeated); a reattached persona gets nothing, because nothing happened to it. Lupin's own restart notices (the managed-bounce warning and all-clear, D61) reach the personas as broadcasts from `lupin`; the kernel's part is what D36 and D55 already give it, reconnect and pull, and nothing more is added here. No broadcast to all personas on a kernel start: that would wake every hibernated one.
*Pros:* the human learns of every restart without being dinged; a persona that saw a failed gate call is told why; hibernated personas sleep through it. *Cons:* one more card kind on the card's `payload` (`kernel_event`), a T27 row of one line.

**Option B: silent.** *Pros:* no noise. *Cons:* a crash loop of the kernel itself would be invisible until someone noticed the fleet was quiet.

**Option C: a broadcast to all personas on every start.** *Pros:* everyone knows. *Cons:* wakes the hibernated fleet for a fact that changes nothing for them.

### 10. The three units

**Option A (recommended): three different dispositions.** `tmux-execve-killtrace.service`: Delete at the Switch clock, with the tmux seats it was written to trace; the `.bt` file and its log line in the runbook go with it. `lupin-cc-memory-watch.service`: Delete (already ruled, D60; listed here only to close the set). `lupin-arbiter-app.service`: stop and disable at Switch, the kernel unit replacing it (already Operate, D63). `lupin-credential-watcher.service`: Operate, outside the harness; it keeps the Lupin *containers* on a live token and has nothing to do with the kernel, which reads credentials from files at use time; its own header says to delete it when the directory-bind mount lands, and that remains its exit. `lupin-bounce-watcher.service`: Operate; it is the :7999 restart button's path and the fleet's warning on a bounce comes through D61's broadcast door, which the kernel receives; nothing to change.
*Pros:* each unit judged by what it touches; two Deletes and two Operates close the T19 row. *Cons:* none worth the name.

**Option B: delete all three at Switch.** *Cons:* the containers lose their token refresh and the bounce button stops working; neither is harness code.

**Option C: keep all three.** *Cons:* a bpftrace probe of tmux with no tmux left to trace.

## Glossary

- **Descriptor store (fd store):** systemd's per-service parking place for open file descriptors, kept across `restart`, dropped across `stop` then `start`.
- **Reattach:** the new kernel taking an engine's three pipes back from the store and resuming the read loop, discarding one partial line.
- **Resume (lazy start):** starting an engine process on a persona's stored conversation when its next block arrives; the same path hibernation uses.
- **Drain:** the bounded wait for a busy persona's turn to end before its engine is stopped.
- **Crash block:** the `[kernel … crashed]` block that opens the first turn after an engine died, carrying the interrupted message when there was one.
- **Job table:** the kernel's list of scheduled jobs with persisted last-run times and catch-up.
- **Register keys named in this narrative:** T08 (this item); D36 (one socket per persona); D50 (credentials); D52 (inbox envelope and ledger); D54 (persona bootstrap, the memento stamp); D55 (Lupin application changes); D56 (log schema and rotation); D57 (context exhaustion); D59 (the fleet); D60 (host capacity); D61 (broadcasts); D62 (turn-end policy); D63 (exit criteria and the switch-over); D64 (the switch-over data migration); T09 (control API); T27 (Lupin application changes).
